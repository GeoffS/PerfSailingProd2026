  <div class="h1">Prototype 5.5m<sup class="sqr">2</sup> Sail Mainsheet Rigging:</div>
  <div class="h2">Last Update: October 16, 2014 - More time sailing with the "mark 3" carbon-fiber prototype</div>
  <hr />
  <p>
    I've always been unhappy with the mainsheet rigging at the clew of the 5.5m<sup class="sqr">2</sup> blokart<sup class="reg">®</sup> sail.
  </p>
  <p>
    I'm a big guy so I tend to sail with the 5.5m<sup class="sqr">2</sup> sail in higher winds than most people; I also use a pretty
    stiff mast most of the time.<br />
    Adding to the problem, I have pretty severe tendonitis in my elbows, so gripping and sheeting-in hard can become
    painful pretty quickly.
  </p>
  <hr />
  <p>
    There are two problems I set out to remedy:
  </p>
  <ol>
    <li>The mechanical advantage of the stock system actually goes below 1:1 when you sheet all the way in (i.e. you need to pull on the sheet
    with more force than the sail is pulling up with).</li>
    <li>You can't sheet the sail down near the end of the pully-whip.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>
    I've been trying to think of a (legal) way to move the block on the sail up and back, while moving the location where the end of
    the mainsheet terminates forward. Ideally you would like to have the pully-whip block "nest" between the forward block and the 
    attachment point.
  </p>
  <p>
    What I came up with is to slide a 3/8" (9.5mm) diameter rod into the loop where the original block attaches to the foot of the sail
    and tie the other end of the rod to the end of the boom. This gives me a way to hang the block and the end of the mainsheet anywhere between the two points.
  </p>
  <p>
    In my <a href="#20140827">first test</a>, I used an aluminium bar I had lying around. You can see it bends a bit even without any wind-load.<br />
    In the <a href="#20140903">"mark 2" version</a> I used something with a larger cross-section to eliminate the bending.<br />
    The <a href="#20140914">"mark 3" version</a> is almost identical to the original test, but with a 10mm dia. pultruded carbon fiber rod that I got
    on E-Bay for $10.
  </p>
  <p>
    One thing you can't see in the photos and video is how much harder I had to pull on the mainsheet with the original rigging.
  </p>
  <p>
    To get an idea of the improvement with the new system, I set a reference at the height I could pull the boom down to with the stock system.<br />
    Then I measured the force necessary to pull the boom down to that point with both systems (no wind load, just the force from
    a standard 900/900/900/600/2nd/tip carbon mast).
  </p>
  <p>
    The stock rigging took about 65-70 lbs.<br />
    The protoype system took between 30-33 lbs.
  </p>
  <p>
    In all fairness, the force in the stock system increases dramatically as you get near the fully sheeted in point (as you would expect given the 
    geometry). Once the boom was out about 12" (30 cm) the sheet force became similar to the protoype's.
  </p>

    <div class="h3">9/14/2014 - The "mark 3" Carbon-Fiber Prototype:</div>
    <p>
      The 10mm dia. pultruded carbon fiber rod arrived last week and I used it for the "mark 3" prototype.
    </p>
    <p>
      I think this may be a "keeper". There was very little flex while sailing. Total cost (not including the Nautos blocks): about $13.

    <div class="h3">9/3/2014 - The "mark 2" Prototype and the First Sailing Experience:</div>
    <p>
      I was pretty sure the 3/8" (9.5mm) aluminum rod wouldn't hold up to real sailing forces, so I made the "mark 2" prototype from 
      some thin-wall aluminum square tubing I got at the local hardware store. It's a bit clunky and the block is a bit lower than it would be
      with a smaller support, but it lets me sail with the new geometry without waiting for any new parts.
    </p>
    <p>
      Everything worked <b>great</b>. The sheet was much easier to pull and the sail-shape looked pretty much unchanged from stock.
    </p>
